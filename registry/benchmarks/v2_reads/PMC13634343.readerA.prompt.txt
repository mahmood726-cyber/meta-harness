TITLE: Routine versus deferred percutaneous coronary intervention in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation: a frequentist and Bayesian meta-analysis of randomized trials with trial sequential analysis. 
PUBLICATION TYPES: ["review-article", "Review", "Journal Article"]
ARTICLE TYPE: review-article
ABSTRACT:
Abstract Background The optimal management of concomitant coronary artery disease (CAD) in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) remains debated. Therefore, we performed this meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) to compare routine percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) versus deferred PCI in patients with severe aortic stenosis and significant coronary artery disease undergoing TAVI. Methods We systematically searched major electronic databases for eligible RCTs. The primary efficacy outcome was spontaneous myocardial infarction (MI); the primary safety outcome was major bleeding. Bayesian random-effects meta-analysis models were fitted using weakly informative prior distributions. Effect estimates are reported as posterior median risk ratios (RR) with 95% credible intervals (CrI). The posterior probability of benefit (PPB) for efficacy outcomes or harm (PPH) for safety outcomes was calculated (RR < 1 or RR > 1, respectively). Results Three RCTs comprising 1156 patients were included. Routine PCI did not demonstrate a convincing posterior probability of benefit for spontaneous MI (posterior median RR 0.84, 95% CrI 0.43–1.73; PPB: 70.2%) or all-cause mortality (posterior median RR 0.91, 95% CrI 0.60–1.43; PPB: 69.6%). However, routine PCI was associated with a reduced risk of urgent revascularization (posterior median RR 0.37, 95% CrI 0.15–0.91; PPB: 98.1%). For safety outcomes, routine PCI was associated with an increased posterior probability of harm for major bleeding (posterior median RR 1.56, 95% CrI 0.91–2.71; PPH: 95.6%) and life-threatening or disabling bleeding (posterior median RR 1.74, 95% CrI 1.00–3.07; PPH: 97.4%). Conclusions Among patients undergoing TAVI with concomitant CAD, routine PCI did not reduce ischemic events or mortality but increased bleeding complications. These findings suggest that a selective revascularization strategy, rather than a routine one, may be preferable. PROSPERO registration CRD420261386909. Keywords: Coronary artery disease, Percutaneous coronary intervention, Randomized controlled trial, Routine PCI, Transcatheter aortic valve implantation

METHODS:
2. Methods 2.1. Study design and registration This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the methodological framework established by the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [13] and reported in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [14] . The study protocol was registered prospectively in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration identifier: CRD420261386909). 2.2. Search strategy A comprehensive systematic literature search was executed in PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception through April 4, 2026, without language restrictions. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms for (“transcatheter aortic valve implantation” OR “TAVI” OR “TAVR” OR “transcatheter aortic valve replacement”) AND (“percutaneous coronary intervention” OR “PCI” OR “coronary revascularization” OR “stenting”) AND (“randomized controlled trial” OR “RCT” OR “randomized”). The detailed search strategies for each database are presented in Table S1. 2.3. Eligibility criteria We included RCTs that enrolled adult patients with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis and concomitant significant CAD, as defined by each trial (Table S2), who were undergoing TAVI and that compared a strategy of routine planned PCI (performed before, during, or shortly after TAVI) versus a strategy of no planned PCI, defined as conservative management or deferral of PCI until clinically indicated. We excluded observational studies, including cohort studies, case-control studies, case series, and case reports, as well as review articles, editorials, commentaries, and study protocols without results. 2.4. Endpoints The primary efficacy endpoint was spontaneous MI. The primary safety endpoint was major bleeding. Secondary endpoints were all-cause mortality, cardiovascular death, any MI (spontaneous and procedural), stroke (any type), any revascularization, urgent revascularization, any bleeding, minor bleeding, life-threatening or disabling bleeding, acute kidney injury (AKI), and trial-defined MACE. Functional outcomes were also assessed using the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class (I-IV) at 12 months after TAVI and the Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) angina class (0-IV), with higher classes indicating greater symptom severity. Detailed endpoint definitions for each included trial are presented in Table S3. 2.5. Study selection and data extraction Duplicate records were removed using Mendeley Reference Manager, and the remaining records were screened using Rayyan systematic review software. Two reviewers (A.E and M.A) independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text assessment against the predefined eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third author (L.S). Reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles were hand-searched to identify additional eligible trials not captured by the electronic database search. Two independent reviewers (A.E and A.M) extracted data using a standardized data collection sheet. Extracted information included trial characteristics (first author, year of publication, trial name, number of centers, countries, enrollment period, sample size, and definition of significant coronary artery disease), patient characteristics, procedural characteristics, and outcome events (number of events and total patients in each arm for all outcomes of interest). All data were subsequently revised by a third author (H.A) for accuracy. 2.6. Risk of Bias and certainty assessment Two reviewers (M.A. and A.M.) independently evaluated the risk of bias for all included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB-2) tool [15] . Assessment covered five domains: the randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, incomplete outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and bias in selection of the reported results. For each outcome, the certainty of evidence was appraised independently by the same reviewers using the GRADE approach. Evidence from randomized trials was initially rated as high and could be downgraded based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Certainty was classified as high, moderate, low, or very low. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached [16] . 2.7. Statistical analysis All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) with the `meta` and `bayesmeta` packages. Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) was performed using TSA software version 0.9.5.10 Beta (Copenhagen Trial Unit). 2.7.1. Bayesian analysis Primary analyses were performed under a Bayesian random-effects meta-analytic framework to better characterize parameter uncertainty given the small number of included trials. Weakly informative priors were specified: a normal prior for the log risk ratio (RR) (mean = 0, SD = 2) for the pooled effect parameter (μ), and a half-normal prior for the between-study heterogeneity standard deviation (τ) with scale = 0.5 [17] . Pooled effect sizes are reported as posterior median RRs with 95% credible intervals (CrIs). For efficacy endpoints, we calculated the posterior probability of benefit (RR < 1) and clinically meaningful benefit (RR < 0.8). For safety endpoints, we calculated the posterior probability of harm (RR > 1) and clinically meaningful harm (RR > 1.25). Under the Bayesian framework, effect estimates are partially pooled toward the overall mean, a phenomenon known as shrinkage, which reduces extreme estimates from small or imprecise studies, thereby producing more stable and clinically plausible effect sizes compared to conventional meta-analytic estimators. To assess the robustness of our findings to the prior specification for τ, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using alternative heterogeneity priors: a half-normal distribution with scale = 1.0, a half-Cauchy distribution with scale = 0.5, and a half-Cauchy distribution with scale = 1.0, all compared against the primary half-normal (scale = 0.5) prior. Posterior estimates from all sensitivity models were compared to evaluate the influence of the heterogeneity prior on the pooled effect size and CrIs. 2.7.2. Frequentist analysis Secondary frequentist meta-analyses were performed under a random-effects framework using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimator to quantify between-study variance (τ 2 ). This approach was selected over the DerSimonian-Laird method due to its reduced bias and superior coverage properties when the number of included studies is small [18] . Given that conventional random-effects models may yield overly narrow confidence intervals with few included trials, conventional 95% CIs were complemented with 95% CIs calculated using the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman [HKSJ] adjustment as a sensitivity analysis [19] , [20] . Pooled effect sizes were expressed as RRs, calculated via the inverse-variance weighting method. In instances of zero events in both intervention arms, a standard continuity correction of 0.5 was applied to all cells of the affected contingency table to permit variance estimation. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the I 2 statistic. According to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, I 2 values of 0%–40% may represent unimportant heterogeneity, 30%–60% moderate heterogeneity, 50%–90% substantial heterogeneity, and 75%–100% considerable heterogeneity [13] . For I 2 values that fell within overlapping ranges, both applicable descriptors were reported. Formal assessment of small-study effects, including Egger's regression test and visual inspection of funnel plot asymmetry, was precluded due to the inclusion of only three trials, as such diagnostics are unreliable and lack sufficient statistical power when fewer than ten studies are available [21] . A two-sided p < 0.05 threshold defined statistical significance. 2.7.3. Sensitivity analysis We also performed sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the MACE estimate. First, we excluded the NOTION-3 trial because its definition of MACE included urgent revascularization as a component of the composite. Revascularization is a “softer” endpoint that can be influenced by ascertainment and treatment bias, whereas the other trials defined MACE using harder clinical endpoints. Second, we restricted the analysis to 1-year follow-up to harmonize follow-up duration across trials. For this analysis, we used hazard ratios (HRs) as reported in the ACTIVATION and PRO-TAVI trials [12] , [22] . For NOTION-3, which did not report 1-year HR [1] , we reconstructed time-to-event data from the published Kaplan-Meier curves using the method described by Li et al. [23] to estimate the 1-year HR for MACE. 2.7.4. Trial sequential analysis Trial sequential analysis (TSA) was performed to adjust for the risk of random error due to sparse data and repetitive testing in cumulative meta-analyses for spontaneous MI, bleeding outcomes (any bleeding, major bleeding, and life-threatening bleeding), and revascularization outcomes (any revascularization and urgent revascularization) [24] , [25] . The required information size (RIS) was calculated assuming a 5% (two-sided) type I error rate, 80% power, the observed event proportion in the control arm, and the anticipated relative risk reduction from the pooled estimate. To account for between-study heterogeneity in the required information size calculation, a diversity-adjusted model was used, which incorporates the D 2 statistic to inflate the required information size when heterogeneity is present. Trial sequential monitoring boundaries were constructed. For outcomes where the cumulative Z -curve crossed the trial sequential monitoring boundary for benefit or harm, the finding was considered conclusive regardless of whether the diversity-adjusted required information size (DARIS) was reached. For outcomes where the Z-curve did not cross the boundary, and DARIS was not reached, the evidence was deemed inconclusive and potentially subject to type II error.
